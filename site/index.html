      How GEOS works

      Environment is not exposure. The same 42 °C afternoon endangers a new hire in a double-layer suit but not an acclimatised supervisor in the shade. GEOS works out what is happening to each person and warns on the forecast, not the damage.

      SensorsWeather, wearable heart rate, personal gas
      1 MonitorSpike, dropout and stuck-sensor checks
      2 DetectWBGT heat load, sudden dust or gas changes
      3 AssessCore temperature, strain, gas dose per person
      4 WarnForecast, reasons and actions, on the device first

      Predict with the environment, correct with the body

      Prior

      A heat-strain model driven by WBGT, clothing and workload, anchored to NIOSH limits, sees heat building before the body shows it.

      Correction

      Heart rate corrects it through a Kalman filter (Buller et al. 2013). A second state learns each worker's own drift.

      Graceful failure

      If a strap drops out, the filter keeps predicting, widens its uncertainty and says so. It never fails silent.

      Alerts people trust

      • Predictive: WARNING when the forecast reaches 38.5 °C within 20 minutes; DANGER near 39 °C.
      • Sticky: three samples to escalate, twelve calm ones to step down. No flapping.
      • Explainable: every alert carries its reasons and a concrete action, including a NIOSH work/rest split.
      • Dose, not just level: H2S uses a 10-minute dose from the personal monitor, because the area sensor can read fine while one person is not.

      Honest limits

      • The benchmark is synthetic. It is not clinical validation.
      • Heart-rate estimates are weaker for very fit or heavily acclimatised people.
      • WBGT assumes the ground is at air temperature, which under-reads radiant heat from hot desert ground. Decision support, not a medical device.
      References
      • Liljegren et al. (2008). Modeling the Wet Bulb Globe Temperature Using Standard Meteorological Measurements. J Occup Environ Hyg 5(10).
      • NIOSH (2016). Criteria for a Recommended Standard: Occupational Exposure to Heat and Hot Environments.
      • Buller et al. (2013). Estimation of human core temperature from sequential heart rate observations. Physiol Meas 34(7).
      • Moran, Shitzer, Pandolf (1998). A physiological strain index to evaluate heat stress. Am J Physiol 275.
      • ILO (2024). Ensuring safety and health at work in a changing climate. NIOSH/OSHA H2S limits: 10 ppm 10-min REL, 20 ppm ceiling, 100 ppm IDLH.
